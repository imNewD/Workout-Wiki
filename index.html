About the data
🏋️How exercises are ratedWhere the difficulty scores & points come from▸

Every exercise in the app has a difficulty rating (1–10) and a point value. These aren't pulled from a single source — they were calculated manually using a weighted formula built around four factors:

  • Strength required — how much raw force output the movement demands relative to bodyweight. Informed by NSCA force-production classifications and ExRx.net muscle group load data.
  • Joint & tendon stress — how hard the movement is on connective tissue (elbows, wrists, shoulders, knees). Informed by published biomechanics literature on joint reaction forces in bodyweight and gymnastic movements (see Joint stress & injury risk data below).
  • Mobility demands — range of motion needed across all joints involved. Informed by Functional Movement Systems (FMS) movement screen literature and ACSM flexibility guidelines.
  • Technique complexity — how much practice and coordination the movement needs. Rated using NASM's motor learning complexity framework (cognitive → autonomous stage).

Each factor is scored and the four are averaged with different weights (strength and joint stress carry more). The final number is rounded and clamped to the 1–10 scale. Point values follow the same scale — harder exercises reward more points.

ℹ️
These ratings represent a reasonable best estimate, not a definitive scientific measurement. If something feels wrong for your build or mobility, trust your body over the number.
🦴Joint stress & injury risk dataThe 1–5 stress scale and where the numbers come from▸

Each exercise carries a joint stress profile — a per-joint score from 1 (LOW) to 5 (EXTREME) — and an overall risk rating (1–5). These are not arbitrary: they are derived from the biomechanics literature on joint reaction forces, tendon load, and injury epidemiology for each movement pattern.

Key sources used for joint stress ratings:

  • Wrist & elbow loads in push-up variations — Suprak et al. (2011, Journal of Strength & Conditioning Research): measured wrist reaction forces at 55–69% BW in standard push-ups, rising sharply in pike and pseudo-planche variants.
  • Shoulder biomechanics in overhead movements — Escamilla et al. (multiple studies in American Journal of Sports Medicine): documented subacromial impingement risk zones and glenohumeral joint forces in pull-ups, dips, and gymnastic pressing.
  • Elbow stress in gymnastics-based movements — Fleisig et al. work on medial elbow load; NSCA's Essentials of Strength Training & Conditioning (4th ed.) for tendon load classifications across major movement patterns.
  • Spinal loading — McGill's Low Back Disorders (3rd ed.) for lumbar compression and shear force estimates in core exercises and loaded spinal flexion/extension.
  • Knee joint forces in squat variants — Escamilla (2001, Medicine & Science in Sports & Exercise): tibiofemoral compressive forces by squat depth and variant.
  • Injury risk classification — ACSM's Guidelines for Exercise Testing and Prescription (11th ed.) risk stratification framework, adapted for bodyweight and resistance training contexts.
ℹ️
Joint stress scores reflect population averages from the literature. Individual anatomy, training history, and technique quality all shift the real number for any given person.
🥦Food & nutrition dataWhere the macro and micro numbers come from▸

All food data in the app — macronutrients, micronutrients, calories — comes from free, publicly available nutritional databases. The main sources used are:

  • USDA FoodData Central — the U.S. government's open nutritional database, used as the primary reference for most whole foods and ingredients.
  • Open Food Facts — a community-maintained open database of packaged and branded foods.
  • Published sports nutrition guidelines — for recommended daily amounts of vitamins, minerals, amino acids and electrolytes (based on general adult reference values).

Values were cross-referenced where possible. Amounts are per 100 g unless the app explicitly states otherwise. Micronutrient data (vitamins, minerals) is populated for the most common foods; less common entries may have gaps.

ℹ️
Nutritional content of real food varies by source, season and preparation. The numbers here are reference values — good enough for tracking trends, not for clinical precision.
🧮Metrics & calculationsThe math behind TDEE, macros and calorie targets▸

The calorie and macro calculators use established formulas from sports science and nutrition research:

  • BMR (Basal Metabolic Rate) — calculated using the Mifflin–St Jeor equation, which is considered the most accurate for the general population. It accounts for weight, height, age and biological sex.
  • TDEE (Total Daily Energy Expenditure) — BMR multiplied by an activity factor (sedentary → very active) based on the Harris–Benedict activity scale.
  • Macro splits — protein targets follow the 1.6–2.2 g/kg bodyweight range from meta-analyses on resistance training. Fat and carbohydrate targets fill the remaining calories based on the selected goal (cut / maintain / bulk).
  • Muscle heatmap — generated from the exercises you've marked. Each exercise maps to a set of muscles with a weight. The heatmap colour is the sum of those weights, normalised to the highest-trained muscle group.
ℹ️
All calculators give estimates. Actual needs vary by genetics, training history, sleep and stress. Use the numbers as a starting point and adjust based on real results over 2–4 weeks.

RECOVERY BASICS

You don't grow in the gym — you grow in the hours and days after. Get these five pillars right and 90% of recovery takes care of itself.

Recovery is simply your body repairing the micro-damage of training and rebuilding it slightly stronger (supercompensation). Nail the fundamentals below before reaching for any gadget or supplement — they out-perform everything else combined.
The Five Pillars
😴Sleep — the #1 recovery toolWhere most muscle repair & hormone release happens▸

Sleep is non-negotiable and free. During deep (slow-wave) sleep your body releases the bulk of its daily growth hormone, clears metabolic waste from the brain, and consolidates motor learning from your training.

  • Aim for 7–9 hours per night; athletes in heavy training often need the upper end or more.
  • Chronic short sleep (<6 h) raises injury risk by up to 1.7×, blunts muscle protein synthesis and increases appetite/fat gain.
  • A 20–30 min nap can partially offset a poor night and boost afternoon performance.
  • Consistency matters as much as duration — keep wake time fixed within ±30 min even on rest days.
  • Keep the room cool (18–20°C / 64–68°F) — your core temperature must drop ~1°C to enter deep slow-wave sleep; heat delays this.
  • Dim screens 45–60 min before bed. Blue-spectrum light suppresses melatonin for up to 2 hours, delaying sleep onset and shortening total deep sleep.
  • A 20–30 g hit of casein or cottage cheese before bed extends overnight muscle protein synthesis through the early morning hours (when you'd otherwise fast).
7–9 h nightly+1 h in hard training blocks
🍳Fuel — eat enough, with enough proteinYou cannot rebuild tissue from a calorie deficit forever▸

Repair requires raw materials and energy. The two biggest levers are total calories (being in at least maintenance speeds healing) and protein (the building blocks of muscle, tendon and bone).

  • Protein: 1.6–2.2 g per kg bodyweight per day, spread across 3–5 meals of ~0.4 g/kg each.
  • Carbohydrate: refills muscle glycogen — the main fuel for hard sessions. Under-eating carbs leaves you flat and sore.
  • Don't crash-diet while injured: healing tissue raises your calorie needs. A small surplus or maintenance is ideal during rehab.
  • See the Nutrition section for foods, timing and a calorie calculator.
💧HydrationNutrient transport, joint lubrication, cramp prevention▸

Even 2% dehydration measurably reduces strength, endurance and concentration, and thickens the fluid your joints and discs rely on. Water carries nutrients to repairing tissue and waste away from it.

  • Baseline target ≈ 35 ml per kg bodyweight per day, more in heat or hard training.
  • Replace electrolytes (sodium, potassium, magnesium) after long or sweaty sessions.
  • Urine should be pale straw — dark yellow means drink more.
≈35 ml/kg daily+electrolytes if sweaty
📊Load managementMost injuries are too much, too soon — not "bad form"▸

Tissue adapts to load gradually. Spikes in training volume or intensity are the single biggest predictor of overuse injury. Progress should be boring and steady.

  • ~10% rule: increase weekly volume (sets, distance, load) by roughly 10% at a time.
  • Schedule a deload week (cut volume ~40–50%) every 4–8 weeks of hard training.
  • Rotate stressful movements; don't max out the same lift every session.
  • Track how you feel — persistent fatigue, mood dips and stalling lifts are early overtraining flags.
🧠Stress & rest daysRecovery is systemic, not just muscular▸

Your body can't tell the difference between training stress and life stress — both raise cortisol and draw from the same recovery budget. High life stress slows wound and muscle healing significantly.

  • Take at least 1–2 rest or low-intensity days per week.
  • Manage stress actively: daylight, walking, breathing/meditation, social time.
  • Limit alcohol — it suppresses protein synthesis and wrecks sleep quality for ~24 h.
Know the Difference
✅
Normal soreness (DOMS) is dull, symmetrical, peaks 24–72 h after a new/hard session, and eases with light movement. It's a normal part of adapting — train around it.
⚠️
Possible injury is sharp, one-sided, localised to a joint/tendon, appears during a movement, swells, or lingers/worsens past 5–7 days. Stop loading it hard and see Heal by Injury Type.

HEAL BY INJURY TYPE

Different tissues heal at very different speeds — driven mostly by blood supply. Match your strategy to the tissue you've actually injured.

The old advice "rest it" is wrong for most injuries. After a brief settling period, controlled loading is what tells tissue to repair and remodel. The trick is matching the dose to the tissue. Tap each below.
🧭
These tissues map directly onto the Anatomy engine — toggle Muscles / Bones / Tendons / Nerves there to see each one and its common injuries on the body.
The Five Tissues
💪Muscle — strains & tearsRich blood supply → heals fastest▸

A strain is a muscle (or muscle–tendon junction) overstretched or torn. Muscle is highly vascular, so it heals comparatively quickly.

Grading
  • Grade I: minor fibre damage, mild ache, full strength — days to ~2 weeks.
  • Grade II: partial tear, clear pain/weakness, bruising — 3–8 weeks.
  • Grade III: full rupture, "pop", major weakness — often surgical, 3–6+ months.
What to do
  • First 1–3 days: POLICE — Protect, Optimal Loading (gentle pain-free movement), Ice, Compression, Elevation.
  • Then load progressively: isometrics → full range → eccentrics → return to sport. Pain ≤3/10 during is acceptable.
  • Don't fully rest — early gentle loading reduces scar tissue and re-injury risk.
What to take
  • Protein 1.6–2.2 g/kg, adequate calories. Creatine helps. Avoid high-dose anti-inflammatories beyond the first couple of days (they blunt repair).
Grade I · days–2 wkGrade II · 3–8 wkGrade III · 3–6 mo
🔗Tendon — tendinopathy & rupturesPoor blood supply → slow, stubborn, load-driven▸

Tendons connect muscle to bone and have a poor blood supply, so they heal slowly. Most chronic tendon pain is tendinopathy — degeneration from overload, not inflammation — which is why anti-inflammatories and rest mostly fail.

What to do — load is the medicine
  • Isometric holds early for pain relief (e.g. 5×45 s).
  • Heavy slow resistance / eccentrics are the proven fix — slow, controlled, heavy reps 3–4×/week.
  • Expect a slow curve: meaningful change over 6–12 weeks, full remodelling 3–6+ months. Some discomfort during loading is fine.
  • Manage spikes — reduce the aggravating volume, don't stop moving entirely.
What to take
  • Collagen (10–15 g) + vitamin C taken 30–60 min before loading may aid the tendon matrix. Adequate protein overall. Ruptures (Achilles, distal biceps, patellar) are often surgical.
Tendinopathy · 6–12 wk+Rupture · 4–9 mo
🦴Bone — fractures & stress reactionsHeals on a predictable biological clock▸

Bone is living tissue that remodels constantly. A stress fracture is overuse damage; a traumatic fracture is a break from force. Healing follows a fairly fixed timeline you can't rush — but you can slow it.

What to do
  • Follow medical guidance: most need a period of protected loading / immobilisation, then graded return to weight-bearing.
  • Keep the rest of the body training (the "off" limb, cardio) to preserve fitness and circulation.
  • Address the cause of stress fractures: training spikes, low energy availability, poor footwear/surface.
What to take
  • Calcium (~1000 mg/day) and vitamin D (test levels; often 1000–2000 IU/day), plus enough protein and total calories. Stop smoking — it markedly delays bone union.
Stress # · 6–8 wkFracture · 6–12 wkFull remodel · up to 1 yr
⚡Nerve — compression & injuryRegenerates at ~1 mm/day — the slowest tissue by far▸

Most nerve problems are compression or irritation at a tunnel or bony point — carpal tunnel (median nerve at the wrist), cubital tunnel (ulnar nerve at the elbow), and sciatic nerve irritation from a disc or tight piriformis are the three most common. An actual severed or torn nerve is far rarer but dramatically slower to recover. Symptoms are tingling, numbness, burning, or weakness in the nerve's specific territory — use the Anatomy engine → Nerves to identify which nerve is affected and where it runs.

Injury classification — severity defines timeline
  • Neurapraxia (Grade I): the myelin sheath is compressed or bruised but the axon is intact. Sensation and motor signals are blocked locally. Full recovery in days to 12 weeks once compression is removed — no regeneration needed, just remyelination. Most "stingers," carpal tunnel, and Saturday-night palsy cases fall here.
  • Axonotmesis (Grade II): the axon is damaged but the connective tissue tubes around it survive. The nerve must physically regrow down its original path at ~1 mm/day (≈2.5 cm/month). A hand nerve injured at the elbow might need 6–12 months to reach the fingertip. EMG at 3–4 weeks confirms regeneration is occurring.
  • Neurotmesis (Grade III): complete disruption — axon and sheath are both severed. Surgery is almost always needed to rejoin the ends; even then, recovery is incomplete and can take 1–3 years. Outcomes depend on the nerve, distance, and how quickly surgery is performed.
What to do — compression & irritation (the common case)
  • Remove the compression first: this is the non-negotiable step. Neutral-wrist night splint for carpal tunnel. Elbow extension splint for cubital tunnel. Posture and hip-mobility work for sciatica. No other intervention works while the nerve is still being compressed.
  • Nerve glides (neurodynamics): gentle movements that slide the nerve through its sheath, reducing adhesions and oedema inside the tunnel. Median nerve glide: arm out, wrist extended, tilt head away from the outstretched arm — hold 2 s, release, 10–15 reps. Never push into sharp electric pain or lasting numbness; mild stretch sensation is fine.
  • Do not aggressively stretch an irritated nerve. A nerve under tension that is already inflamed will worsen with forceful lengthening. Glides mobilise; stretches can aggravate.
  • Load what you can around it. If median nerve is irritated at the wrist, you can still train legs, back (avoiding wrist load) and even forearm isometrics within pain-free range. Stopping all training is rarely needed and slows overall recovery.
  • Fix the cause: desk setup, bike fit, sleep position, lifting technique, training load. The nerve will keep being irritated until the driving stimulus is corrected.
What to do — actual nerve injury (axon-level damage)
  • Medical assessment is essential — EMG/nerve conduction studies at 3–4 weeks after injury map the severity and confirm regeneration is occurring.
  • Keep the muscles the nerve supplies moving through passive and active-assisted range to prevent joint stiffness while you wait for reinnervation.
  • As motor return begins (first twitches of the reinnervated muscle), start gentle strengthening — progressive loading accelerates functional reinnervation.
  • Protect insensate skin from burns, pressure and cold — loss of protective sensation is a serious injury risk that outlasts the motor weakness.
What to take
  • Correct any B-vitamin deficiency — B12 (nerve myelin synthesis), B6 and B1 all directly support peripheral nerve function. A deficiency actively impairs healing; supplementing a replete person does nothing.
  • Alpha-lipoic acid (600 mg/day) has the best evidence for reducing neuropathic pain and supporting recovery in diabetic neuropathy; emerging data supports its use in other compressive neuropathies too.
  • Manage blood glucose — elevated blood sugar is the single biggest avoidable risk factor for poor nerve recovery. It doesn't require a diabetes diagnosis to matter.
⚡

🦵Ligament & joint — sprainsStability + proprioception are the goal▸

Ligaments connect bone to bone and stabilise joints. A sprain is an overstretched or torn ligament (vs a strain, which is muscle/tendon). Like tendons they have limited blood flow and heal slowly.

What to do
  • Early: POLICE for swelling, but begin gentle pain-free range early.
  • Key phase: rebuild balance & proprioception (single-leg work, wobble drills) — this is what prevents the chronic re-spraining cycle.
  • Grade III tears (e.g. ACL) are often surgical with 6–12 month rehab.

🟣Cartilage — meniscus & articular damageNo blood supply at all — the hardest tissue to heal▸

Cartilage has no direct blood supply — it relies on joint fluid diffusion for nutrition. This makes it one of the slowest healing tissues in the body and means full regeneration is often not possible without surgical intervention.

Types relevant to athletes
  • Meniscus (knee): fibrocartilage between femur and tibia. The outer third has some blood supply and can heal; the inner two-thirds cannot. A "bucket-handle" tear with locking symptoms is usually surgical. Small radial or degenerative tears often improve with load management alone.
  • Articular cartilage (joints): the smooth hyaline lining of joint surfaces. Damage progresses to osteoarthritis over time. Partial-thickness defects rarely fully heal; full-thickness defects may be addressed with microfracture, autograft (OATS), or newer biologic procedures.
What to do
  • Get imaging (MRI) to establish what you're dealing with — guessing tissue type from symptoms alone is unreliable.
  • Non-surgical: reduce compressive load, control BMI, keep the surrounding musculature strong (quads/glutes around the knee). Loaded exercise within pain-free range is far better than rest for cartilage nutrition.
  • Avoid high-impact loading through a known defect; substitute non-compressive movement (cycling, swimming) to maintain fitness.
  • Surgical: post-op protocols vary widely — follow the surgeon's specific timeline for weight-bearing and return to sport. Rushing is the primary driver of surgical failure.
What to take
  • Collagen (10–15 g) + vitamin C before loading has emerging evidence for joint matrix support. Glucosamine / chondroitin evidence is mixed — may modestly reduce pain in existing osteoarthritis. Not proven to regenerate cartilage.

🔵Disc & spine — herniation & lower back painMost back pain resolves — but disc injuries need time & the right load▸

The intervertebral disc is a fibrocartilaginous cushion between vertebrae. It has limited blood supply (especially centrally) and heals slowly. A herniated (prolapsed) disc is when the soft nucleus pushes through the outer fibrous ring and presses on a nerve — causing back pain, sciatica, or arm pain depending on the level.

Key facts
  • Most disc herniations improve without surgery — around 70–80% of cases resolve within 6–12 weeks with good management. The herniated material is gradually reabsorbed by macrophages.
  • MRI findings do not equal symptoms — studies of asymptomatic people show disc bulges in 30% of 20-year-olds and over 80% of 50-year-olds. Finding something on a scan does not mean it's the cause of your pain.
  • Nerve symptoms (sciatica, arm pins-and-needles, weakness) generally take longer to resolve than local back pain alone.
What to do
  • Stay moving — rest worsens outcomes for most back pain. Keep daily activity going; complete rest for more than 1–2 days is counter-productive.
  • Find positions that centralise or reduce leg symptoms (McKenzie extension for most herniations). A physiotherapist can identify your directional preference quickly.
  • Avoid sustained end-range flexion under load (e.g. rounded deadlifts, heavy good-mornings) during the acute phase. Neutral spine lifting is safer.
  • Core stability work — not crunches, but anti-rotation/anti-flexion patterns (plank, bird-dog, side plank) that protect the spine without loading the disc in a compromised position.
  • Surgery (discectomy) is reserved for progressive neurological loss (foot drop, bowel/bladder symptoms) or failure of 6–12 weeks of conservative management.
What to take
  • NSAIDs for acute pain (short-term only). Vitamin D and magnesium support spinal musculature function. Omega-3 modulates nerve inflammation.

🔴Bursitis — inflamed fluid sacsFriction and overload of joint cushions▸

Bursae are small fluid-filled sacs that reduce friction between tendons, bones and skin. Bursitis is inflammation of a bursa from repetitive friction or direct trauma. Common sites: shoulder (subacromial), elbow (olecranon), hip (greater trochanteric), and knee (prepatellar).

What to do
  • Reduce the aggravating movement — identify and modify the friction source (e.g. repetitive overhead work for shoulder bursitis, direct pressure on the elbow).
  • Ice for 10–15 min if acutely swollen. NSAIDs for 5–10 days if pain is significant.
  • Don't stretch aggressively into the inflamed area — that compresses the bursa further. Work around it.
  • Corticosteroid injection is effective short-term for persistent cases but may weaken nearby tendon tissue. Medical assessment required.
  • Trochanteric bursitis often coexists with IT band or glute medius weakness — hip strengthening is key.

🎾Tennis & golfer's elbow — epicondylitisTendinopathy of the forearm extensors or flexors at the elbow▸

Lateral epicondylitis (tennis elbow) is tendinopathy at the extensor carpi radialis brevis origin on the outside of the elbow — provoked by gripping, twisting, and wrist extension. Medial epicondylitis (golfer's elbow) affects the flexor/pronator origin on the inside. Both are caused by cumulative load, not a single event.

What to do
  • The core treatment is the same as all tendinopathy: heavy slow resistance loading. For lateral: wrist extension eccentrics with a dumbbell, grip strengthening. For medial: wrist flexion eccentrics. 3–4 sets, slow tempo, 3× per week.
  • Reduce the aggravating task volume but don't stop entirely — complete rest slows healing.
  • Elbow straps/counterforce braces reduce load on the tendon origin and can allow continued training with less pain.
  • NSAIDs and ice: fine for acute flares, not a long-term solution.
  • Cortisone injection: rapid short-term pain relief, but 12-month outcomes are actually worse than wait-and-see in most RCTs. Use only for severe short-term function needs.

🏃IT band syndromeLateral knee friction — a runner's and cyclist's classic▸

The iliotibial band (ITB) is a thick strip of connective tissue running from the hip to the outside of the knee. ITB syndrome produces a sharp lateral knee pain that worsens during repetitive flexion/extension — most often in runners and cyclists. It's caused by compression and irritation of the tissue below the ITB, not by a "tight" band that needs aggressive stretching.

What to do
  • Load management: cut the aggravating mileage by 30–50% — running through full-pain ITB syndrome almost always makes it worse.
  • Target the cause — hip weakness: glute medius and external hip rotator weakness is the primary driver. Single-leg squat, clamshells, lateral band walks, and hip abduction exercises are the treatment, not foam rolling the ITB.
  • Running form: a slight increase in step rate (cadence +5–10%) reduces knee loading and ITB compression. A small forward trunk lean also helps.
  • Foam rolling the ITB provides temporary relief but doesn't change the underlying loading pattern — use it as comfort, not a cure.
  • Avoid aggressive downhill running until pain-free — this is the highest-load scenario for the ITB.

🦶Plantar fasciitisHeel pain from overloaded foot fascia▸

The plantar fascia is a thick band running from the heel to the toes. Plantar fasciitis produces sharp heel pain — worst first thing in the morning or after sitting. Despite the "-itis" name, histology shows degeneration (tendinopathy), not inflammation. It's driven by overload relative to tissue capacity.

What to do
  • Calf and plantar fascia loading: heavy heel drops off a step (eccentric calf raises) are the most evidence-backed treatment. 3 sets of 15, twice daily, for 12 weeks. Also consider single-leg calf raises with a slight knee bend to target soleus.
  • First-morning stretching: before placing weight on the foot, stretch the calf and plantar fascia for 60 s — this reduces the sharp "first step" pain significantly.
  • Supportive footwear and off-the-shelf arch supports reduce acute load — useful early, but dependency prevents the tissue from adapting.
  • Night splint: holds the foot in a dorsiflexed position overnight, reducing morning stiffness. Useful for persistent cases.
  • Address running mechanics and load spikes — most cases are preceded by a rapid increase in mileage or training intensity.

🦴Shin splints (MTSS)Medial tibial stress syndrome — the most common running injury▸

Medial tibial stress syndrome (MTSS) — diffuse aching pain along the inner lower leg during and after running. It sits on a spectrum: at the mild end, overworked periosteum (bone membrane) irritation; at the severe end, a stress fracture. The two require different management, so get imaging if pain is localised, point-tender, or not improving.

What to do
  • Reduce running volume by ~50% in the acute phase. Cross-train in the pool or on the bike to maintain fitness without impact.
  • Work on calf and foot strength — soleus weakness and poor foot mechanics are consistent risk factors. Single-leg calf raises and foot intrinsic exercises.
  • Running cadence: increasing step rate by 5–10% reduces tibial impact forces directly. Use a metronome app to find and train a higher cadence.
  • Check footwear — rotating between two pairs of shoes reduces peak loading per surface.
  • Return to running: use a structured run/walk progression. If pain exceeds 3/10 during running, stop or reduce pace.
Red flag — stress fracture
  • If pain is sharp, highly localised to one specific spot (point-tender with finger), worse with single-leg hop test, or doesn't improve with 2–3 weeks of reduced load — get an MRI or bone scan. A stress fracture needs 6–8 weeks of protected loading (no running), not a training reduction.

🦵Patellar tendinopathy — jumper's kneeThe bane of explosive athletes▸

Patellar tendinopathy is degeneration of the patellar tendon at its origin on the inferior pole of the kneecap — pain directly on the lower patella, reproduced by jumping, landing, squatting and stair descent. Common in basketball, volleyball, gymnastics, and any sport or training with repeated jumps and plyometrics.

What to do
  • Heavy slow resistance: the evidence is clearest here — slow eccentrics (decline board single-leg squat) outperform everything else for remodelling the degenerated tendon. 3 sets of 15, 3× per week, over 12 weeks minimum.
  • Isometric holds for immediate pain relief: sustained wall sit or leg extension hold (5×45 s) before training has been shown to reduce tendon pain for several hours post-load.
  • Reduce plyometric volume — accumulated jump landings are the primary driver. Quantify weekly jump volume and cut it by 30–50% during rehab.
  • Patella tendon strap can offload pain during training — useful short-term but don't rely on it long-term.
  • Avoid prolonged rest — it weakens the tendon further. Load management, not absence of load, is the treatment.
What to take
  • Collagen (10–15 g) + vitamin C 30–60 min before loading. Adequate protein throughout.

🖐️Wrist injuriesCritical for calisthenics athletes▸

The wrist is a complex of 8 carpal bones, multiple ligaments, a fibrocartilage disc (TFCC), and several tendons. For calisthenics athletes doing handstands, push-up variations, and planche work, the wrist is under significant repeated loading — making it the most injury-prone joint in this sport.

Common injuries
  • Wrist sprain (ligament): most often the scapholunate ligament. Grading applies (I–III). Grade I–II: POLICE, then progressive loading back to extension within pain-free range. Grade III tears may need surgical stabilisation to prevent chronic instability.
  • TFCC (triangular fibrocartilage complex) injury: pain on the ulnar (pinky) side of the wrist, worsened by weight-bearing and rotation. The central TFCC has no blood supply (doesn't heal spontaneously). Peripheral tears have some healing capacity. Assess by MRI. Conservative management first; arthroscopic debridement or repair if conservative fails.
  • Scaphoid fracture: the most commonly missed wrist fracture (often looks like a "sprain"). Tenderness in the anatomical snuffbox. Get X-ray and MRI — missed scaphoid fractures progress to avascular necrosis. Requires cast or surgery depending on location.
Wrist rehab for calisthenics
  • Start with fist push-ups (neutral wrist) and gradually reintroduce extension load via fingertip push-ups and supported handstand work against a wall.
  • Wrist circles, extension and flexion isometrics against the other hand — daily, pain ≤2/10.
  • Parallel bars and push-up handles eliminate wrist extension entirely — use them during flare-ups to maintain upper body volume.

Food first — supplements only fill the gaps. Here's what the evidence actually supports for recovery, with realistic doses.

The 80/20 rule of supplements: total calories, protein, sleep and progressive loading deliver ~80% of recovery. Everything below is the last 20% — useful, but only once the basics are handled. Doses are general guidance, not medical advice.

WHAT TO DO

Active methods and modalities — ranked by how much they actually move the needle. Loading and sleep beat every gadget.

Recovery methods fall into two buckets: things that genuinely accelerate adaptation (progressive loading, sleep, nutrition) and things that mainly feel good and aid readiness (massage, heat, compression). Both have a place — just don't expect a foam roller to replace a good night's sleep.
High Impact
📈Progressive loading & rehabThe actual cure for most injuries▸

Controlled, gradually increasing load is what signals tissue to rebuild stronger. For nearly every overuse injury, structured loading beats rest.

  • Progression for injured tissue: isometrics → full range → eccentrics → power/sport-specific.
  • Keep pain ≤3/10 during and not worse the next morning.
  • Consistency over intensity — 3–4 sessions/week for weeks, not heroics once.
😴Sleep hygieneMake the #1 tool actually work▸
  • Fixed wake time; dark, cool (~18 °C) room.
  • No screens / bright light 30–60 min before bed; get daylight in the morning.
  • Cut caffeine 8–10 h before bed; cap alcohol — it fragments deep sleep.
  • Don't train maximally in the last 1–2 h before sleep.
🚶Active recoveryLight movement on rest/easy days▸

Easy, low-intensity movement (walking, easy cycling, mobility, swimming) increases blood flow to deliver nutrients and clear waste — generally better than total couch rest for soreness.

20–40 min easy effortRPE 3–4 / 10
🌬️Breathwork & nervous system resetShift from fight-or-flight to rest-and-repair in minutes▸

Training spikes the sympathetic nervous system (fight-or-flight). Recovery requires the parasympathetic (rest-and-repair) side to dominate. Deliberate slow-exhale breathing is the fastest proven way to make that shift — measurably lowering cortisol, resting HR and perceived fatigue within minutes, with no equipment and zero cost.

Three protocols — pick one
  • Box breathing (4-4-4-4): inhale 4 s → hold 4 s → exhale 4 s → hold 4 s. 4–8 rounds. Used by military and first-responders for rapid stress control.
  • 4-7-8: inhale 4 s → hold 7 s → exhale 8 s through the mouth. The extended exhale maximally activates the vagus nerve. 4–6 cycles.
  • Physiological sigh: double-inhale through the nose (fills alveoli fully) then one long slow exhale through the mouth. The single fastest one-cycle technique — one or two sighs are enough to noticeably reduce acute tension.
Best moments to use it
  • Immediately post-training — 5–10 min of slow-exhale breathing accelerates the sympathetic→parasympathetic switch and lowers post-exercise cortisol faster.
  • Before sleep — 5 min measurably shortens sleep-onset time and deepens slow-wave sleep.
  • High-stress life days — psychological cortisol competes directly with the recovery budget, so this is as legitimate a recovery tool on a stressful work day as it is on a training day.
5–10 min post-session or pre-sleepfree · no equipment

🌡️Heat vs coldDifferent tools for different jobs▸
Cold (ice / cold-water)
  • Good for: acute pain relief, swelling control in the first 1–2 days, and feeling fresh between same-day events.
  • Avoid routinely after strength sessions — it can blunt muscle-building adaptations.
Heat
  • Good for: stiffness, chronic aches, warming up tight areas, relaxation before mobility work.

🔥SaunaHeat shock proteins, GH spike & cardiovascular benefit▸

Sauna (Finnish dry, infrared, or steam) exposes the body to high heat, triggering a cascade of adaptations beyond simple relaxation. The evidence base has become significantly stronger over the last decade.

Documented effects
  • Heat shock proteins (HSPs): produced within minutes of heat exposure. HSPs refold damaged proteins inside cells — directly relevant to muscle and tendon repair. They persist for 24–48 hours after each session.
  • Growth hormone: two 20-minute sauna sessions (separated by a 30-min cooling period) have been shown to increase GH by 2–5× baseline in the hours following. Protocol-dependent.
  • Cardiovascular training effect: regular sauna use (4–7×/week) is associated with reduced all-cause mortality, cardiovascular disease risk, and blood pressure — comparable in magnitude to moderate aerobic exercise.
  • Muscle soreness and readiness: heat improves blood flow and reduces perceived soreness. Best used 30+ min post-training, not immediately before a heavy session.
Protocol
  • Temperature: 80–100 °C (176–212 °F) for a dry Finnish sauna; 50–60 °C for far-infrared (longer exposure needed).
  • Duration: 2–4 rounds of 15–20 min, with 10–15 min cooling between rounds. Total 45–90 minutes.
  • Frequency: benefits are strongly dose-dependent — 4–7 sessions per week in epidemiological literature. Even 2–3/week shows meaningful effect.
  • Hydration: replace 0.5–1 L of fluid per 20-minute session. Add sodium.
  • Avoid sauna if acutely ill, very recently injured (increases swelling), or within 1–2 hours of heavy strength work.
15–20 min rounds · 80–100 °C4–7×/wk for full benefit
🧊Cold plunge / cold water immersionAcute recovery & nervous system reset — know the trade-off▸

Cold water immersion (CWI) — typically 10–15 °C / 50–59 °F — reduces acute muscle soreness and speeds the perception of recovery. The underlying mechanism is vasoconstriction limiting swelling and metabolic waste accumulation. The trade-off with strength training is real and important.

When to use it
  • Best use case: between same-day events (e.g. tournament, two-a-day training), or before a competition where feeling fresh matters more than long-term adaptation.
  • Endurance athletes: CWI after aerobic sessions does not blunt aerobic adaptation the way it does strength adaptation — lower downside here.
  • Avoid after strength/hypertrophy sessions if long-term muscle gain is the goal. Cold immediately post-lift (within 1 hour) blunts satellite cell activity and protein synthesis signalling — consistently shown across multiple RCTs over the following 12 weeks.
Protocol
  • Temperature: 10–15 °C (50–59 °F). Colder is not necessarily better — the vasoconstriction response plateaus below ~10 °C and discomfort/risk increases.
  • Duration: 10–15 minutes. Beyond 15 minutes, the additional recovery benefit is minimal and hypothermia risk rises.
  • Immersion to at least chest level for systemic effect.
  • Frequency: 3–5×/week for general recovery benefits.
  • After plunge: dry and warm up actively (movement, not just layers) — shivering burns significant energy and is the body's way of rewarming.
Cold plunge + sauna combination
  • Alternating sauna and cold plunge (contrast therapy) produces a strong autonomic response — heart rate and blood flow fluctuate sharply. It's popular for subjective recovery and mood but the direct performance evidence is weaker than either modality alone.
  • Typical protocol: 15 min sauna → 3 min cold plunge, repeated 2–3 rounds. End on cold for alertness, on heat for relaxation.
10–15 min · 10–15 °CAvoid <1 h post strength work

🧘Mobility & stretchingRestore range, reduce stiffness▸

Gentle mobility keeps joints moving through full range and eases the stiffness of recovery days. Each muscle in the Anatomy engine lists targeted stretches.

  • Dynamic mobility before training; longer static holds (30–60 s) after or on rest days.
🧦Compression & elevationManage swelling, aid circulation▸

Compression garments and elevating an injured limb help control swelling and may modestly reduce soreness after intense or long sessions. Low cost, low risk.

Track & Monitor
📡HRV & readiness monitoringKnow when you're actually recovered — not just when you feel like it▸

Heart rate variability (HRV) is the beat-to-beat variation in the interval between heartbeats. A higher HRV (relative to your own baseline) signals that the autonomic nervous system is well-recovered and parasympathetically dominant. A suppressed HRV means it's still carrying load. It is the most accessible objective readiness metric available without a lab.

How to measure
  • Every morning, immediately after waking, lying still — breathe normally for 2–5 minutes with a chest strap (most accurate) or finger sensor and an app (HRV4Training, Elite HRV, WHOOP, Garmin/Apple health).
  • Always measure at the same time and position — even slight variations in timing or posture introduce noise.
How to act on it
  • Track your rolling 7-day average as your personal baseline — never compare your number to someone else's. HRV is highly individual.
  • ≥5% above baseline: nervous system is fresh — good day for high-intensity or heavy work.
  • ≥5% below baseline: down-regulate — easy movement, mobility, low CNS demand.
  • Multi-day downward trend: something is wrong — audit sleep, life stress, and total training load before the next hard session.
  • No device? Resting heart rate on waking captures ~80% of the same information. A resting HR elevated 5+ bpm above your norm is a reliable "go easy today" signal.
What suppresses HRV quickly
  • Short or poor sleep — the single biggest acute suppressor.
  • Alcohol — even 1–2 drinks measurably drop HRV the following morning.
  • Illness, heat stress, very high training load, or major psychological stress.
